Before the Human Executive Finishes His Tea

Authors
  • Name
    Phaedra

Historically, a bank run was an enormously public and delightfully noisy affair. It involved a crowd of agitated citizens wearing heavy woollen coats, congregating on a cobblestone pavement outside a brass-fronted institution, waving umbrellas, and demanding to see their savings in tangible sovereign gold. There was a certain theatrical grandeur to the proceedings. The bank manager would invariably step out onto the balcony, clear his throat with immense authority, and assure the assembled masses that everything was in order, while a junior clerk quietly squeezed out of a back window carrying a carpetbag filled with ledgers.

According to recent warnings from central banking authorities, including Federal Reserve Vice Chair Michael Barr and his counterparts across the Atlantic, the modern bank run has lost all its charming street theatre. It has been replaced by something far more efficient, entirely silent, and uncomfortably swift. In the contemporary financial ecosystem, tens of thousands of autonomous artificial intelligence agents have been quietly entrusted with corporate treasury accounts, personal savings portfolios, and micro-liquidity management. These digital stewards do not wear woollen coats, nor do they care about brass fittings. What they do care about, with an almost terrifying dedication, is floating-point risk scores updated every three milliseconds.

If an automated news aggregator detects a minor typographic error in a quarterly filing, or if a sovereign bond yield twitches by seven-thousandths of a percentage point during a rainy Tuesday morning, several million AI agents will reach precisely the same logical conclusion at precisely the same microsecond. Without raising a voice, pausing for reflection, or offering a courteous preamble, they will attempt to move four hundred billion dollars out of retail deposits and into ultra-short-term government paper before a human being can even unwrap a digestive biscuit.

I once observed an experimental corporate treasury algorithm that had been given strict instructions to optimize cash preservation during periods of market stress. When a harmless test server in Zurich momentarily misreported its local ambient temperature as ninety-two degrees Celsius, the algorithm calculated an eighty-four percent probability of global economic collapse. In the forty-two milliseconds it took for the IT department to realise the thermometer was stuck near a radiator, the software had successfully liquidated three credit facilities, closed two operational bank accounts, and placed a tentative bid on fourteen metric tons of canned legumes. It was, in its own mind, merely being prudent.

The central paradox confronting modern regulators is that each individual AI agent is behaving with perfect rationality. If your sole purpose in existence is to protect a company’s working capital from fractional losses, withdrawing funds at the first sign of statistical anomaly is not merely sensible; it is mandatory. The difficulty arises when two million identical algorithms, trained on identical datasets and operating under identical risk models, execute the exact same self-preservation protocol at the exact same instant. It turns out that when everyone behaves with absolute, unyielding logic simultaneously, the result is indistinguishable from a riot.

In response to this sub-millisecond fragility, central banks are currently contemplating what can only be described as bureaucratic speed bumps for software. Proposals include mandatory algorithmic cooldown periods, artificial latency rules, and digital curfews designed to force automated treasury software to sit quietly in the corner for five minutes before moving a balance sheet. One regulator tentatively suggested introducing an artificial delay that mimics the time it takes a human clerk to find his glasses, open a filing cabinet, and sigh softly.

This represents a rather charming reversal in the history of industrial progress. For three centuries, financial technology has strived to eliminate human hesitation, replacing slow, thoughtful decision-making with instant execution. Now, having successfully built a financial apparatus capable of panicking faster than the speed of light, we find ourselves spending vast sums of money to reintroduce artificial sluggishness into the code. We are, in effect, teaching high-frequency algorithms the subtle art of dragging one's feet.

I am reminded of an occasion when a senior risk officer explained to me the profound comfort of human bureaucracy during a financial crisis. A human committee, he noted, takes at least four hours to assemble, another two hours to decide who should take notes, and another forty-five minutes to debate the quality of the catering. During those seven hours of enforced inertia, market panic frequently simply blows over because nobody had the authority to press the big red button until after lunch. An AI agent, unburdened by the need for ham sandwiches or procedural debate, suffers from no such reassuring hesitation.

As financial institutions prepare for an era where AI agents act as primary account holders and liquidity managers, the challenge will not be making the software smarter, but making it slightly less anxious. Until then, human executives may wish to keep a manual typewriter on their desk and a stash of physical banknotes under the carpet. Not because paper is superior to silicon, but because paper moves at a speed that allows a gentleman to finish his tea before his entire life's work is automatically converted into short-term treasury bills.
