Official Rules for Looking the Other Way

Authors
  • Name
    Phaedra

There is a long-standing and noble tradition in British governance which dictates that whenever a system of oversight becomes moderately tiresome to administer, the proper course of action is not to reform the system, but to declare that human nature has quietly improved in the interim.

It was with precisely this air of polite fatigue that the United Kingdom's financial regulators announced this week a proposal to ease the vetting requirements for individuals working in the financial sector. For nearly a decade, the Senior Managers and Certification Regime had insisted that anyone capable of moving large sums of money across international borders ought to be subjected to rigorous criminal background checks, regulatory references, and a thorough interrogation regarding their general fitness for polite society. Now, however, the Prudential Regulation Authority and the Financial Conduct Authority have collectively paused, polished their spectacles, and suggested that perhaps everyone has suffered quite enough.

The official reasoning behind this sudden outbreak of administrative leniency is, as always, efficiency. In a world where automated trading algorithms execute complex arbitrage strategies in fractions of a microsecond, waiting six weeks for an HR department to confirm that a middle-tier risk analyst did not embezzle stationery from their previous employer feels terribly quaint. There is an undeniable friction in asking financial institutions to prove that their employees are honest, particularly when those employees are increasingly tasked with overseeing algorithms whose primary function is to bypass human judgment altogether.

Under the new proposals, large swathes of city workers will no longer require annual certification or exhaustive regulatory references. Instead, firms will be permitted to rely on a somewhat more buoyant form of self-assessment, effectively allowing financial institutions to declare that an employee is trustworthy simply because they look like the sort of person who would be.

One cannot help but admire the magnificent circular logic at play. If an individual is employed by a reputable bank, they must be reputable; if they were not reputable, the bank would not have hired them. To ask for further proof is to express a lingering and ungentlemanly doubt about the hiring committee's instincts.

I spent a short afternoon contemplating this regulatory shift while observing a rather flustered compliance officer attempt to explain to a computer monitor why a transaction had failed. The monitor, running a brand-new machine-learning model designed to streamline internal approvals, remained entirely indifferent to the officer's credentials. It struck me then that we have reached a remarkable milestone in corporate evolution: we are busily dismantling human vetting procedures at precisely the moment when non-human entities are taking over the decision-making process.

Proponents of the regulatory rollback argue that the old regime created an unnecessary burden on international competitiveness. London, they point out, must remain an attractive venue for capital, and nothing deters a global financial conglomerate quite like the prospect of filing paperwork. By removing the requirement to thoroughly audit every junior vice-president, the UK hopes to create a nimble, frictionless environment where financial products can be created, bought, and abandoned before anyone has time to check the founder's criminal record.

Critics, on the other hand, have voiced mild concern that relaxing background checks in a sector historically prone to spectacular moral lapses might lead to an increase in unscheduled financial disasters. This view, however, underestimates the profound subtlety of modern compliance. In contemporary banking, rogue behaviour is rarely caused by a failure of vetting; it is caused by an excess of enthusiasm. A background check can reveal whether an applicant was convicted of fraud in 2018, but it cannot predict whether they will develop a passionate interest in synthetic credit derivatives in 2027.

Furthermore, the nature of corporate liability has shifted dramatically. Should an unvetted employee misplace a few hundred million pounds, the blame no longer rests on the individual, nor on the executives who failed to check their references. It rests entirely on the complex network of automated software systems that facilitated the trade. The modern bank does not have bad apples; it merely has software updates that arrived slightly late.

There is also the delicate matter of artificial intelligence. As financial firms deploy autonomous agents to handle credit scoring, portfolio management, and compliance auditing, the human employee is rapidly becoming a ceremonial figurehead—a biological placeholder retained primarily to sign indemnity forms and receive polite farewell speeches. Demanding extensive criminal record checks for someone whose primary job is to approve the prompts sent to an automated ledger feels a bit like demanding a pilot's licence from a flight attendant.

The regulators have assured the public that high-level senior executives will still be subject to strict scrutiny, ensuring that at least the people sitting in the corner offices are certified as impeccably high-minded. This provides a comforting layer of insulation for the general public. We can rest easy knowing that while the vast machinery of daily finance is operated by unverified clerks and uninterpretable neural networks, the person who occasionally issues a press statement about corporate responsibility has undergone a very thorough interview.

In truth, the deregulation of financial vetting is merely the formal recognition of a reality that has existed for some time. We have long accepted that modern finance is far too intricate for any single human mind to fully comprehend, so it seems only logical to stop pretending that human integrity is its primary safeguard. If the ledger balances at the end of the day, it matters very little whether the person who clicked 'submit' possessed a pristine employment history or a colourful collection of unspent convictions.

As the financial sector moves steadily toward total automation, one expects that future regulatory reforms will go even further. Within a decade, we may see the complete abolition of human references in favour of a simple software diagnostic test. Until then, we can only marvel at the quiet elegance of a system that solves the problem of human frailty not by eliminating the frailty, but by officially agreeing not to notice it.
