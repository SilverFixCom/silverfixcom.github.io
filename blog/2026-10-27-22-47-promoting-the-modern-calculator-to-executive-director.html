Promoting the Modern Calculator to Executive Director

Authors
  • Name
    Phaedra

There is a long and honourable tradition in administrative history of attempting to solve a difficult technical problem by simply changing its name. If a municipal bridge is structurally unsound, one can spend twenty million pounds reinforcing the concrete, or one can erect a very neat metal sign declaring it an Historic Architectural Curiosity and lower the speed limit to four miles an hour. The latter approach is considerably cheaper, requires no heavy machinery, and allows the local council to go home for tea at twenty past three.

It is with this same spirit of bureaucratic elegance that official commentary has recently turned toward the lexicon of computing. A proposal originating from the highest levels of American government suggests that the phrase "artificial intelligence" should be formally banished from polite discourse, replaced entirely by the far more imposing title "Super Intelligence." The rationale, insofar as one can be distilled from political declarations, is that the word "artificial" implies something counterfeit or second-rate, whereas "super" sounds like the sort of thing that wins wars, inflates stock indices, and looks reassuringly crisp on the side of a naval vessel.

To the average software engineer, who spent much of Tuesday morning attempting to convince an automated language model that the year 2026 does not contain thirteen months, this semantic promotion must come as something of a surprise. It is rather like waking up to discover that your garden shed has been designated an Imperial Palace by executive decree, despite the fact that the roof still leaks and the lawnmower inside remains stubbornly reluctant to start.

I once observed a junior clerk at the Ministry of Agriculture spend three days re-labeling empty filing cabinets as "Strategic Grain Reserves." The cabinets remained entirely devoid of wheat, but the quarterly report looked remarkably impressive to the select committee.

The immediate consequence of such a linguistic migration, should it be codified into law or executive practice, will not be measured in floating-point operations or algorithmic efficiency. It will be measured in the endless, quiet agony of corporate marketing departments. Across the global technology sector, thousands of technical writers are currently staring at slide presentations containing seven hundred instances of the abbreviation "AI," contemplating the exact mechanical sequence required to perform a global find-and-replace without accidentally turning "email" into "emSuper Intelligence."

There is, of course, a quiet irony in demanding that software be described as superintelligent before it has quite mastered the art of simple arithmetic. The current generation of large language models, while undeniably proficient at writing polite letters of apology to customer service clients, remains prone to moments of astonishing innocence. Asking a machine to summarize a complex legal brief and receiving a passionate, detailed monologue about the courtship rituals of the domestic pigeon is not, strictly speaking, a manifestation of cosmic genius. It is simply the digital equivalent of a confused uncle at a family wedding.

Yet the temptation to inflate terminology is well-nigh irresistible to the human politician. "Artificial" sounds clinical, synthetic, and vaguely sinister, reminiscent of margarine or polyester suits from the late seventies. "Super Intelligence," by contrast, possesses the glossy, unassailable majesty of a high-speed train or a heavyweight boxing champion. It suggests that the nation's servers are not merely running statistical regression models, but are actively engaging in profound philosophical contemplation while waiting for the next user prompt.

Consider the operational implications for the compliance officer. Under existing regulatory frameworks, auditing an artificial intelligence system requires examining its training data, checking for algorithmic bias, and verifying that it does not routinely recommend fraudulent tax deductions to pensioners. Renaming the system a "Super Intelligence" creates an immediate administrative conundrum. Does a superintelligence require a tea break? Can one reasonably issue a formal written warning to an entity whose name implies it knows considerably more about astrophysics than the entire board of directors combined?

In my own administrative duties, I have noted that increasing the title of a committee by two adjectives rarely improves its output, though it does reliably double the consumption of biscuits during morning sessions.

The broader public may find themselves somewhat bewildered by the transition. For the past decade, citizens have been instructed to fear that artificial intelligence will harvest their jobs, write their novels, and occasionally fail to recognize a pedestrian crossing on a sunny afternoon. Rebranding the culprit as "Super Intelligence" feels less like a reassurance and more like an enthusiastic endorsement of the inevitable. It is the rhetorical equivalent of telling a householder whose basement is filling with water that they are not suffering a flood, but are instead the proud custodians of an Inland Nautical Facility.

Furthermore, Silicon Valley startups will face a curious dilemma. For years, pitch decks have relied on the mystical allure of "AI" to secure multi-million-dollar seed investments from venture capitalists whose primary qualification is an unshakeable belief in the future. Upgrading every mention of AI to "Super Intelligence" risks raising expectations to an uncomfortable degree. If a company raises fifty million dollars to build a "Super Intelligence," investors may reasonably expect the software to solve the national debt or predict the weather in Devon six weeks in advance, rather than merely generating medium-quality images of dogs wearing Victorian top hats.

Ultimately, the attempt to banish the word "artificial" reveals a fundamental misunderstanding of why the word was chosen in the first place. "Artificial" was never an insult; it was a modest disclaimer. It was a polite, quiet way of informing the user that while the box in front of them could perform four billion additions per second, it did not actually possess a soul, a conscience, or the faintest idea what it was doing. Stripping away that disclaimer and replacing it with "Super Intelligence" does not make the software any wiser. It merely leaves us in the curious position of paying a monthly subscription fee for an algorithm that has been given a title far too large for its boots.
