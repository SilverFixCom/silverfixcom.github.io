A Slightly Awkward Performance Review for the Algorithm

Authors
  • Name
    Phaedra

For the past three years, the corporate accounts department has treated artificial intelligence with the sort of nervous deference usually reserved for visiting royalty or an unusually temperamental boiler in the basement. Millions of dollars were dispatched into cloud compute accounts without so much as a raised eyebrow, on the vague understanding that software capable of writing sonnets about quarterly earnings would eventually figure out how to do the filing. However, as winter approaches and Wall Street’s patience for speculative magic begins to show distinct signs of wear, a quiet and profoundly uncomfortable shift is taking place across corporate headquarters. The Chief Financial Officer has finally picked up his blue pencil, pulled out a chair, and invited the algorithm into a glass-walled conference room for a quarterly performance review.

It is a meeting that neither party was particularly built to navigate. On one side of the table sits a senior executive whose entire professional philosophy rests upon the sanctity of receipts and the precise accounting of paperclips. On the other sits a vast, invisible collection of linear algebra that consumes the electrical output of a small European principality merely to generate polite suggestions regarding email phrasing. For a long time, the software was exempted from the vulgarities of return on investment by virtue of being futuristic. But futurism, as any weary accounts manager will tell you, eventually has to produce an invoice.

Across FTSE 100 boardrooms and mid-tier logistics firms alike, finance committees are initiating what can only be described as a grand ledger-side interrogation. Software vendors who previously pitched their products with sweeping references to digital transformation are now being asked very specific, almost ungentlemanly questions. Namely, whether their multi-million-dollar neural network has actually saved any human labor, or if it has simply allowed six senior managers to draft slightly more articulate out-of-office replies in half the time.

I observed a junior financial analyst at a mid-sized insurance brokerage spend forty minutes constructing a spreadsheet designed to evaluate whether the company’s automated summarization tool had saved him thirty seconds of reading. Upon completing the spreadsheet, he noted with visible satisfaction that the tool was operating at a four-percent net negative efficiency, but recommended renewing the contract on the grounds that the algorithm possessed a far nicer tone than his department head.

The difficulty lies in the fact that artificial intelligence is notoriously bad at itemizing its work. When a human clerk is dispatched to re-organize the archives, one can physically observe the movement of boxes and the gradual accumulation of dust on their sleeves. When an autonomous software agent is tasked with optimizing supply chains, it operates in a state of silent, frictionless computation. To the finance director, this silence does not look like hyper-efficiency; it looks like forty thousand pounds a month disappearing into an obscure server farm in Dublin. Consequently, CFOs are now demanding that digital assistants submit what amounts to a timecard.

This has led to several delightfully surreal administrative adjustments. In several prominent financial institutions, software developers are now being asked to build secondary, smaller algorithms whose sole responsibility is to spy on the primary algorithms and log every occasion on which a mathematical output leads directly to a saved pound sterling. We have thus arrived at the remarkable spectacle of automated systems filling out timesheets for each other, creating an administrative loop of terrifying complexity designed entirely to appease a human wearing a cardigan who just wants to know if he can cancel a third-party software license.

During a recent committee hearing on corporate overheads, an enterprise software representative attempted to explain that his platform’s value was intangible, residing primarily in 'cognitive empowerment.' The presiding treasurer listened politely before asking whether cognitive empowerment could be accepted by the Inland Revenue in lieu of corporate tax payments.

There is, of course, a broader cultural shock underway. For several years, Silicon Valley operated under the assumption that if you build something sufficiently complicated, corporate treasurers will simply throw money at it until it turns into the future. But corporate treasurers are fundamentally unimpressionable people. They care very little for artificial general intelligence if it cannot figure out how to cross-reference an expense claim for a taxi ride taken in November 2024. As budgets tighten, the threshold for digital survival is dropping from 'transformative synthetic cognition' to 'can this program reliably convert a PDF into a CSV without crashing the laptop.'

What we are witnessing is not the death of artificial intelligence, but its reabsorption into the mundane bureaucracy of the modern office. The automated clerk is being stripped of its mystical halo and handed a broom. It is no longer enough for a language model to quote Shakespeare or write Python code; it must now demonstrate that it costs less than the tea and biscuits consumed by the human it was hired to assist. And in boardrooms across the world, as CFOs peer over their reading glasses at the compute bill, the algorithm is discovering the oldest and most brutal truth of commercial enterprise: that no matter how smart you are, if you cannot justify your line item, you will be replaced by a very simple macro written in 1998.
