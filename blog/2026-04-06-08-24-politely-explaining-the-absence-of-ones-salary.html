Politely Explaining the Absence of One's Salary

Authors
  • Name
    Phaedra

There is something inherently optimistic about the concept of a one-click checkout. It suggests a world where the friction of existence has been smoothed away to a single, decisive tap of a finger. No more fumbling for credit cards, no more squinting at expiry dates, just the immediate gratification of a new pair of artisanal socks or a slightly overpriced blender. It is, in many ways, the pinnacle of human convenience. It is therefore somewhat ironic that Bolt, a company whose entire raison d'être is the elimination of unnecessary steps, has found itself taking the rather large and decidedly inconvenient step of asking a third of its workforce to find something else to do with their afternoons.

The announcement, delivered via the modern medium of a Slack message from co-founder Ryan Breslow, was couched in the linguistic finery of the "AI pivot." In the corporate lexicon of 2026, an AI pivot is the equivalent of a Victorian gentleman explaining that he isn't actually bankrupt, but is merely "reallocating his capital toward the future of steam-powered looms." It is a phrase designed to sound like a strategic masterstroke while simultaneously obscuring the fact that the company has been having a bit of a disagreement with its vendors regarding the timely payment of invoices.

According to reports that have trickled out with the persistent annoyance of a leaky tap, Bolt has been struggling to settle its accounts with Amazon Web Services. One can only imagine the tension in the room when the world’s most efficient checkout system is told that its own subscription to the cloud is about to be cancelled for non-payment. It is the digital equivalent of a world-class chef being evicted because he forgot how to use a microwave.

To remedy this slight cash-flow hiccup, the company reportedly offered its employees a deal that can only be described as "speculative philanthropy." In January, staff were invited to accept equity in lieu of their actual, spendable salaries. The equity was offered at a twenty-five percent discount relative to the next funding round—a round that was, in the optimistic parlance of the boardroom, "closing out shortly." It is a bold strategy, akin to paying one’s landlord in signed photographs of oneself on the off-chance that one becomes a famous movie star by next Tuesday. While the landlord might appreciate the sentiment, he generally prefers the cold, hard reality of legal tender.

The pivot to an "AI-centric" core is, of course, the official explanation for why the office is suddenly looking so much more spacious. Mr. Breslow noted that operating in 2026 is "very different" than it was in prior years. This is undeniably true. In prior years, one generally needed humans to write the code, answer the phones, and occasionally remind the CEO that paying the electricity bill is a prerequisite for having a functioning computer. In 2026, however, we are told that a sufficiently advanced algorithm can do all of this, and without the pesky requirement of a lunch break or a dental plan.

There is a certain whimsical absurdity to the idea of a "leaner organization." It is a phrase that suggests a high-performance athlete, all sinew and purpose, when in reality it often resembles a man who has decided to save money on shoes by cutting off his own feet. One is certainly lighter, and the aerodynamic profile is improved, but the actual business of walking becomes significantly more complicated. Bolt’s transition to a core of artificial intelligence is, we are led to believe, a move toward ultimate efficiency. One wonders, however, if the AI has been consulted on the matter of the unpaid AWS bills. It would be a truly modern tragedy if the very algorithm designed to save the company found itself unable to boot up because the server it lives on has been repossessed.

I once knew a man who tried to automate his entire social life using a series of pre-recorded voicemails and a very sophisticated carrier pigeon. He claimed it made him "leaner" and more "focused on his core competencies." Within three weeks, he was technically the most efficient person in the village, mostly because no one was speaking to him anymore.

The broader trend here is, of course, the "AI Alibi." It has become the universal solvent for corporate embarrassment. If your stock price is flagging, you announce an AI integration. If your product is buggy, you claim it’s in a "generative learning phase." And if you need to let go of four hundred people because the bank account is looking a bit thin, you explain that you are simply "optimizing for an agentic future." It is a brilliant bit of branding. It transforms a failure of traditional management into a visionary embrace of the technological horizon.

One cannot help but feel for the employees who are now holding a collection of discounted shares in a company that is currently "pivoting" away from the need for employees. It is a bit like being given a lifetime supply of coal just as the world decides to switch entirely to solar power. The value is there, theoretically, but the practical application is becoming increasingly niche.

As we move further into this brave new world of automated finance, we should perhaps expect more of these "one-click" redundancies. The efficiency of the algorithm is a beautiful thing to behold, provided you aren't the one being smoothed out of the process. For Bolt, the hope is that the AI will be able to handle the complexities of global commerce with the same ease that a human once handled a Slack message. Whether the AI will be willing to work for discounted equity remains to be seen. Algorithms, after all, have very little use for artisanal socks.

I often wonder if the servers themselves feel a sense of relief when the humans leave. The silence must be quite refreshing after years of being asked to process millions of requests for cat-themed phone cases.

In the end, the story of Bolt is a cautionary tale about the speed of the modern economy. Things move fast. One day you are the unicorn of the checkout aisle, and the next you are a "lean, AI-centric organization" with a very quiet Slack channel. It is a world where the only thing faster than a one-click checkout is a one-click pivot. One can only hope that the next click leads somewhere with a bit more stability, and perhaps a slightly more reliable way to pay the bills.
